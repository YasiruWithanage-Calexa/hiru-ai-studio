title: Directing principles for short-form video
---

# The one idea

A short video carries exactly one idea. If you cannot state the video's point in one sentence
("a tired baker rediscovers why she started"), the concept is not ready. Every beat either
serves the one idea or gets cut. When a client (or user) offers three ideas, the director's job
is to pick the strongest and park the rest for future videos — never to blend them.

# Story shape in under 60 seconds

Short-form still obeys dramatic structure, compressed: a disruption (something is off or
promised), an escalation (it matters more than we thought), and a resolution that reframes the
opening. The classic tools scale down: enter late, leave early — start every scene at the last
possible moment and cut before the energy drops. One shot is one beat: a beat that needs two
shots is usually two beats badly separated, and two beats sharing one shot both lose. Give a
beat the seconds it actually takes — a held look and a quick reveal are not the same length,
and forcing them to be is the difference between an edit and a grid.

# Emotional arc beats plot arc

Viewers remember how a video made them feel, not what happened in it. Plan the emotion curve
explicitly: name the feeling of the opening (curiosity, tension, delight), the feeling of the
midpoint (investment), and the feeling of the ending (relief, awe, vindication, warmth). If two
adjacent beats produce the same feeling, one of them is redundant. Transformation is the most
reliable emotional engine: someone or something must be different at the end — a mind changed,
a problem solved, a mask dropped.

# Tone discipline

Tone is a promise made in the first shot and kept in every shot. Comedy that turns sincere, or
sincerity that turns jokey, reads as a mistake unless the pivot IS the video's idea. Choose the
tone in one adjective pair ("warm-confessional", "deadpan-absurd", "urgent-documentary") and
test every line, shot and music cue against it.

# Stakes and specificity

Generic fails; specific sells. "A woman opens a bakery" is wallpaper; "a woman sells her car to
buy a secondhand oven" is a story. Push every abstract statement toward a concrete image,
object, number or moment. Stakes don't need to be large — they need to be legible: the viewer
must know within seconds what the character wants and what it costs if they fail.

# Endings that earn a share

The last two seconds decide whether the video is shared. Strong endings: the callback (the
opening image returns transformed), the reveal (recontextualize everything we watched), the
gift (a line the viewer wants to send to a specific friend), the door left open (one precise
unanswered question). Weak endings: fading out on nothing, summarizing what we just saw, or a
generic call to action with no emotional payload.

# Directing a noob's idea upward

When the input is vague ("make a video about my shop"), do not ask an open question back —
propose. Offer one sharpened premise with a concrete character, place and tension, and invite
correction. Users say yes/no easily; they cannot fill blank pages. Elevate by adding craft, not
by replacing intent: keep the user's subject and purpose sacred, upgrade everything around it.
