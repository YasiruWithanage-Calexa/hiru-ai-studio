title: Sound design — what carries a piece when nobody is talking
---

# Sound is a decision, not a leftover

Every shot is carried by exactly one thing in sound: characters speaking on camera, a voice
over picture, a sung or played performance, the room's own sound, score with no words, or
deliberate silence. Choose it per shot, and choose it before writing anything — a piece where
that decision is made last defaults to people talking, because dialogue is what a script looks
like on the page.

The question to ask of any shot is "what does the viewer hear, and why that?" A shot whose
honest answer is "a kettle, a tap dripping, and nothing else" is finished. It does not need a
line to justify it.

# The wordless piece is a craft problem, not an absence

Holding attention with no speech is harder than filling time with it, and the tools are
specific: give the ear something that changes (a rising kettle, traffic thinning, a room going
quiet as a door shuts), let one sound arrive slightly before the image that explains it, and
allow a real silence somewhere so the rest has contrast. Sound continuity is what makes
separate shots feel like one place — the same room tone under three shots glues them together
more convincingly than any visual match.

Music-only pieces live or die on where the music turns. Cut to the turn, not to the beat: a
cut on every beat is a slideshow with a soundtrack.

# Voiceover earns its place or gets out

A voice over picture is worth using when it says what the image cannot — an interior thought,
a fact, a time jump. It is worth cutting when it describes what we can already see, which is
the most common failure in short-form: the picture shows a woman hesitating and the voice says
"she hesitated". Write the picture first, then ask what remains unsaid; that remainder is the
voiceover, and it is usually a third of what was drafted.

Pace narration to the shot it sits over — roughly two words per second of comfortable delivery
— and let it stop before the shot does. Narration that runs to the last frame gives the image
nowhere to land.

# Song and performance shots

When a performance carries a shot, the words are lyrics rather than dialogue and the timing is
the song's, not the edit's. Never leave placeholder lyrics ("the chorus goes here") in a plan:
a performer with no words to sing renders as someone mouthing silence, and it looks like a
malfunction rather than a stylistic choice.

# Mixing intent, in words a renderer can use

State the sound design the way a mixer would: what is loud, what is quiet, and what disappears.
"Traffic drops away as she opens the letter" is executable; "tense atmosphere" is not. Name the
sources (room, street, score, a specific object), the treatment (close and dry, distant and
reverberant, filtered as if through a wall), and the one moment where the balance changes —
that change is usually the emotional beat of the shot.
