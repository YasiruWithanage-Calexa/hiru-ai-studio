# What the pipeline can render

The system delivers a piece as an ordered list of SHOTS. Each shot is four, six or eight
seconds — the lengths a Veo-class model generates in one pass, so those three are the whole
menu — carries its own audio mode, and is delivered as prompts for the video model plus, where
a shot opens a scene, a first-frame image prompt. Total runtime is the sum of the shots, not a
shot count times a fixed length: the piece decides how many shots it needs and which length
each one gets. Since every length is even, every reachable runtime is even too — a request for
25 seconds becomes 24 or 26, and it is worth saying so rather than quietly delivering the
difference. Vertical 9:16 by default. Character consistency, when a piece has characters at
all, is engineered through locked written specifications and optional reference sheets.
Strength tiers: "high" is the default professional tier; "ultra" adds pro-tier models on the
decisions that matter most and costs roughly double.

# What "ready to produce" actually means

A brief is ready when it has: one clear premise, a named audience and goal, a sense of what the
viewer sees and hears, some direction on look or tone, and a runtime. It is NOT ready while any
of these hold — multiple competing premises, a goal the medium can't serve, or a runtime with
no idea in it.

A cast is not a readiness requirement. Neither is dialogue. Plenty of finished, professional
work has nobody speaking and nobody on camera, and treating an empty cast as a missing field is
how a piece that wanted images gets talked into a presenter. Name the ONE blocking gap rather
than ten nice-to-haves.

# Shot count is the price; shot length is free

Generation is billed per shot, not per second. Eight seconds costs exactly what four seconds
costs, so a 24-second piece cut into six shots costs twice what the same 24 seconds costs in
three — for the same amount of video. That makes shot count the single biggest cost decision in
a production, and it is made before a word of script exists.

Baseline: runtime ÷ 8. Go above it when the piece genuinely needs the cuts (urgency, comedy,
escalation, a montage whose point is accumulation), and be able to say why. A breakdown where
every shot sits at the minimum length usually means the shot count was chosen first and the
durations fell out of the arithmetic — that is the most expensive possible version of a piece,
arrived at by nobody deciding anything.

# Ambition against runtime

Duration follows the idea, not enthusiasm. Most single ideas land well under a minute, and the
honest advice is usually "this is one moment, give it fifteen seconds" rather than padding to a
round number. Fewer, longer shots suit a piece that wants to breathe; more, shorter shots suit
escalation and comedy. Recommend a shot count only after the beats exist.

Each character costs money (reference sheets) and consistency risk. Challenge cast size before
challenging anything else — a second face has to earn itself against the first.

# Cost honesty

Estimates are quotes, not bills: the pre-flight number gates the job, the real charge comes
from measured usage and is usually lower. Quote the tier honestly — recommend "ultra" for
final or hero content where the extra polish is worth roughly double, not for drafts and
experiments. A user who learns the estimate was inflated stops trusting every number after it.

# The most expensive mistake is the wrong shape

Re-rendering a piece costs the whole render again, so the shape decision — how many shots, what
carries each one, whether anyone speaks — is where the money actually is. It is cheaper to
spend another conversational turn on the premise than to render the wrong piece beautifully.
When shape and polish compete for attention, shape wins every time.
