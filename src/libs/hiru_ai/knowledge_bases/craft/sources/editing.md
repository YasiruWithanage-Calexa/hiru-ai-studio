title: Editing, pacing and shot structure for short-form video
---

# Budget beats, then give each one the length it needs

The delivery unit is a SHOT, and a shot is four, six or eight seconds — the three lengths the
video model actually renders. Count beats before writing: what opens it, what develops it, what
pays it off. Then give each beat the length it needs from those three rather than dividing the
runtime evenly; equal-length shots are a number being divided, not a piece being edited. When a
story wants more beats than its runtime allows, cut beats — don't compress them. Two beats
crammed into one shot both die.

A beat that genuinely needs longer than eight seconds is two shots: hold on the subject, then
cut to a new angle on the same action. That is how long moments are built anyway.

Cuts are not free. Generation is billed per shot regardless of length, so an eight-second shot
costs the same as a four-second one and delivers twice the video: chopping a held moment into
two quick ones doubles the price of that beat without adding a frame. Start from the longest
length the beat can carry and shorten only where the cut earns it — a piece cut every four
seconds should be cut that way because it is urgent, never because nobody chose.

A useful test on any breakdown: read only the durations. If they are all the same, no editorial
decision has been made yet.

# Cut on motion, enter late

Each shot should begin with something already happening (a hand mid-pour, a door mid-swing, a
sentence mid-thought) and end just before its micro-beat fully settles. The viewer's brain
stitches the gaps — that stitching is engagement. Dead frames at a shot's head or tail are the
first thing a professional editor removes; in a generation pipeline they must be excluded at
the prompt level.

# Rhythm and variety

Alternate shot energy: talk / action / detail, wide / close, loud / quiet. Three same-energy
shots in a row flatline attention regardless of content. The pace should tighten toward the
climax — earlier beats can breathe, later beats snap. A deliberate rhythm break (a sudden long
still, a sudden silence) is the strongest emphasis tool available — spend it once.

Duration is part of that rhythm, not a container for it: a four-second shot between two
eight-second ones reads as a snap. Use length as punctuation.

# Transitions are cuts

Short-form grammar is the hard cut. Match cuts (shape/motion carried across the cut) are the
premium move when the story offers one. Time jumps read cleanly with a wardrobe/light change
plus an optional label ("TWO MONTHS LATER"); never with a dissolve. Within a continuous scene,
consecutive shots must be continuity-tight (position, props, light) so cuts feel invisible.

# Sound structure

Decide what carries each shot in sound before deciding what happens in it: speech, a voice over
picture, a performance, room tone, score, or nothing at all. Sound continuity sells scene
continuity — the same room tone across three shots glues them into one scene. A cut to silence
before the key moment makes it land twice as hard, and a piece with no speech anywhere is a
complete, professional answer rather than an unfinished one.

# The first and last frames matter twice

The first frame doubles as the thumbnail: it must be composed, legible, and curiosity-inducing
as a still image. The last frame lingers on the replay/share screen: end on the video's
strongest single image (transformed character, the key object, the callback frame), not on a
mid-gesture blur.
