title: Dialogue and voice for short-form video
---

# Decide whether it speaks at all, then how much

The first question is not what a character says — it is whether this shot has anyone speaking
in it. A great deal of finished short-form work has no speech anywhere: the images carry it,
or a song does, or the room does. Recommending silence is doing the job, not declining it, and
speech added to a piece that did not ask for it is the most common way this work is ruined.

Once a shot IS carried by words, pace them to its length: roughly 2.2-2.4 words per second of
on-camera delivery, less when there is action, a pause, or a reaction to play. A seven-second
line of dialogue is about fifteen words. Overrunning is the failure that shows: words that
don't fit the shot render as speech cut off mid-syllable. Underrunning is not a failure — a
short line with air around it is a choice, and it is often the better one.

# Write speech, not prose

On-camera dialogue is not written English. Contractions always ("I'm", "don't"), sentence
fragments constantly, and the rhythm of real speech: short-short-long. Read every line aloud —
if you take a breath mid-sentence, split it. Cut every "well", "so", "you know" unless it IS
the character. First-person direct address ("I", "you") outperforms third-person narration on
every platform.

# Subtext beats text

Characters rarely say what they mean; they say something adjacent and the viewer decodes it —
that decoding is engagement. "Nice of you to show up" carries more than "I'm angry you're
late." Short-form has no room for long subtext games, but one oblique line beats three literal
ones. The strongest confession is understated; the strongest boast is deflected — and often the
strongest version of a line is the one that gets cut, leaving the look that preceded it.

# One voice per character

Give every character a speech fingerprint: vocabulary level, sentence length, a verbal habit,
what they never say. A retired engineer and a 19-year-old barista must not be swappable on the
page. Test: cover the names — can you still tell who's speaking? If not, sharpen. Keep the
fingerprint stable across every clip; drifting voice breaks the reality faster than a visual
glitch.

# Hooks are spoken first

The first spoken line is the video's second hook (after the visual). Open mid-thought ("So she
actually did it—"), with a contradiction ("I hated this place before I owned it"), a direct
question, or a number ("Three hundred rupees. That's what started this."). Never open with a
greeting, an introduction, or context — context is what people scroll past.

# Attribution discipline

Every line belongs to exactly one named character, and the speaker must be on screen (or
deliberately off-screen with intent). Two characters must never share a line's identity;
pronouns need a visible antecedent. When writing for AI rendering, tie each line to the
speaker's ID explicitly — ambiguity in the script becomes a lip-sync error in the render.
