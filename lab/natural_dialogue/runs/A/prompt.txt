Night, inside a modest middle-class home in Colombo, Sri Lanka. A dining table under a warm ceiling light, plastic chairs, a calendar on the wall.

Anudi, a Sri Lankan woman in her mid-twenties with long black hair in a low ponytail, sits across the table from her father, a Sri Lankan man in his late fifties with greying hair and a plain white shirt. She has just told him that Kasun, the young man who sells food from a three-wheeler on the road, is only her friend. He does not believe her. Worried and a little hurt, not shouting, he leans forward and presses her, looking straight at her:
"යාළුවෙක් විතරද? ඒ ළමයට මොනවද තියෙන්නේ? තමන්ගේ කියලා හරියට තැනක්වත් නෑ."
The first question is doubtful, almost a scoff; the second is sharper; the last trails off quietly as he shakes his head and looks away. Anudi listens with her eyes down, turning her phone in her hands; she does not speak.

They speak casual, everyday spoken Sinhala, the way a real family talks at home in a Sri Lankan teledrama: not an interview, not a speech, nobody looks at the camera.

Camera: a vertical medium shot over Anudi's shoulder onto her father, handheld with a slight natural sway.

Sound: only their voices in the room, a ceiling fan turning, crickets outside. No music.
