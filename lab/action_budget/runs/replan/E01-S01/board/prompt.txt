A cinematic storyboard sheet for a Sri Lankan television drama: 12 photorealistic film stills of ONE scene, laid out in a grid 4 panels across and 3 down, read left to right, then top to bottom. Every panel is a landscape 16:9 still of the same size, separated by thin, even, plain white gutters. All panels are the same moment's world, painted together: the same people, props, weather, light and colour grade in every panel.

People (every panel): every person is a smooth, matte, faceless artist's mannequin in one solid colour, with jointed limbs and simple hands: Kasun is the red mannequin; office worker is the blue mannequin. A small raised ridge on the front of the head, where a nose would be, shows which way the head faces. Each mannequin has the person's real height and build and sits, stands and holds things exactly as a real person would. No faces, no hair, no clothes, no skin: only the mannequin colour. Everything else (the place, the props, the light, the weather) is a real, photorealistic film still.

World (every panel): A single painted storyboard image with twelve equal panels, four across and three down, read left to right. Early morning Colombo daylight is warm and low from screen right, with soft canopy shade, restrained colour and muted urban surroundings throughout. All people are smooth artist's mannequins of consistent adult proportions: Kasun red, the customer blue, and incidental distant people neutral grey. The humble food three-wheeler has dull green bodywork, a shallow faded canvas canopy, a chipped green canopy upright and a scratched steel counter. Props are rendered realistically: a stainless tea flask with a dark handle and removable lid, a dented kettle, a metal tray of short eats, a plain ceramic tea cup and a beige cleaning cloth. The service cup is concealed before its first appearance at the customer edge of the counter. Enclosed traffic vehicles have obscured occupants. No lettering, panel labels, captions, symbols or arrows appear anywhere.

Geography: The three-wheeler stands along the pavement, with its body and recessed preparation bay to screen left and the Colombo road opening to screen right. The counter projects from the shaded bay toward the customer standing area; a narrow inner work surface returns into the vehicle. The chipped canopy upright marks the left side of the serving opening, while the same road and kerb continue along the right. With no supplied axis, the roadside master establishes Kasun left and the customer right: the customer approaches across frame toward the left and departs across frame toward the right. Most cameras stay on this roadside side of their exchange; the elevated view observes the same arrangement from above. The interior reaction camera stands just inside the bay, offset from Kasun's working line, retaining a wedge of road brightness at frame right. The final customer-side oblique view preserves the green vehicle left and road right.

Bodies (every panel): each person sits or stands the way a real person does the task in front of them, never posed for the camera. The body faces the task and only the head turns: someone at a steering wheel sits squarely behind it, the wheel centred in front of the chest, back against the seat, both hands on the rim; someone at a desk sits square to it; someone at a stove faces the stove. A camera beside a person sees the body side-on, not the chest. Nobody looks into or faces the lens unless the panel says so. Head and eyes turn together: the eyes never roll hard to one side while the face points at the camera; a near-frontal face looks just past the lens.

Draw NO text of any kind: no panel numbers, no captions, no titles, no timestamps, no arrows, no labels, no borders other than the gutters. The people, place and everyday details are Sri Lankan.
