Action budget. Every clip has an action budget, counted in actions, never in seconds: a clip lasts as long as its actions take. A HARD action is a change of state the video model must invent: a door, gate, lid or umbrella opens or closes; a person gets into or out of a vehicle, sits down or stands up; a thing is taken, picked up, put down, placed, tucked, passed, poured, dropped or falls; clothes go on or off; a vehicle stops or pulls away. A MOVE is a simple visible movement: walks, turns, looks, nods, smiles, leans, reaches. A state (keeps, holds, remains, still in her hand) is not an action. The budget of ONE clip: (1) at most ONE hard action; (2) at most THREE actions in all, hard and moves together; (3) never a hard action while a person walks or travels in the same clip (the phone slips while she walks on; she steps out and walks off; the umbrella opens mid-walk). A moment that needs more is several clips joined by the cut, the way television dramas cover it: each hard action gets its own clip, and the next clip OPENS on its result (the door already open, the person already standing on the pavement, the umbrella already open, the cup already in his hand), so the model never has to make the change happen twice. Write each beat's action as one action: never chain "opens the door, steps out and pushes it closed" in one beat. A routine of the hands (lift the flask, pour, set it down, take the cup) is several clips or one chosen hard action with the rest already done in the first frame. Leave nothing half done: what a cut skips must still add up. A door, gate or boot that is opened (or is already open) when a person gets out or goes through is closed on screen before that person walks away: plan the closing as its own clip with the person standing still (she pushes the rear door shut beside the car; a small nod to the driver may go with it), and the walk away as the next clip. Never leave a car door open behind someone who walks off. A clip that carries a spoken line OPENS with the speaker already turned so the camera reads the face (three-quarter, profile or frontal, eyes off the lens), never facing away, into the vehicle or with the back to camera: the first frame is the video model's picture of that face. A turn toward the camera before the line is the end of the clip before, or happens before the clip opens; the speaking clip opens on its result.
